# Spreadsheets and records

## What this source contains

- Ledgers, registers, and logs
- Exports from the systems the office runs on (accounting, customer, stock, payroll)
- Form responses and survey results
- Change logs or audit columns that record who edited a row and when

Records show what was happening when the item changed. They answer "where did this number come from".

## How to search it

1. **Find the record set** the item draws on or reacts to. Confirm its columns before you trust a name.
2. **Bound every query by date.** Start with the weeks before and after the item changed.
3. **Look at the values before the change.** A threshold that equals the largest or the typical value from that period was probably chosen from the data.
4. **Look for a step.** A count that jumps or drops to zero near the change date ties the change to an event.
5. **Read the change log** for the rows the item depends on.

## What good evidence looks like here

- A figure in the item that equals a computed value from the records of that period
- A problem that shows in the records before the change and stops after it
- A change log entry by the item's author on the same day

## Common pitfalls

- **Recorded is not caused.** A matching number is circumstantial. Pair it with a message or a note before you claim cause.
- **Definitions change.** A column may have meant something else when the item was written.
- **Exports are snapshots.** A file exported today may not show the values as they stood then.
- **Missing periods.** If the records don't reach back far enough, that is a gap, not a null result.

## What to return

For each relevant finding:
- The file or system, the sheet or table, and the exact filter or formula you used
- The date range
- A short numeric summary (counts, totals, largest, first and last dates). Don't paste raw rows.
- How it lines up in time with the item's change
- Strength: direct, circumstantial, or weak
